① Gaussian scale space — L(x,y,σ) = G(σ) * I
σ (sigma) — blur intensity
σ is the Gaussian standard deviation in pixels. Larger σ = heavier blur = finer detail erased.
5 σ values per octave: 1.0 → 1.26 → 1.59 → 2.0 → 2.52
Each step × k = 2⅓ ≈ 1.26. After 3 steps, σ doubles.
Octave — resolution level
One octave = 5 blurred images at a fixed resolution. To begin the next octave, take the σ = 2.0 image and halve the width and height (÷2). The same σ sequence then repeats at the smaller size.

❓ Common question: why do we do both blurring and halving resolution? Isn't that the same effect?
Both reduce visible detail — but they serve completely different purposes. SIFT needs both to be accurate and fast.
🔬 Blurring = fine, continuous scale steps Blurring simulates viewing from further away without changing the pixel count. SIFT uses these small steps to find features at finely-spaced scales inside each resolution level.
📐 Halving resolution = big jumps, cheap compute Without downsampling, detecting large features would need a massive 121×121 blur kernel on the full-resolution image — enormously slow. After halving, a tiny 5×5 kernel has the same mathematical effect as a 10×10 on the original.
✅ Result: best of both worlds Blurring = continuous fine-tuned sensitivity. Halving = kernels stay small and fast. Remove either and SIFT either misses features or grinds to a halt.
② Difference of Gaussians — D(x,y,σ) = L(kσ) − L(σ) ≈ σ²∇²G
Subtract adjacent blurred images within each octave. This approximates the Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) — a blob/edge detector — at a fraction of the cost.
5 images per octave → 4 adjacent pairs → 4 DoG per octave × 4 octaves = 16 DoG images
The bright halos and dark centres reveal blob-like structures at that scale level.
↓
③ 26-neighbor extrema detection
Why 26 neighbors across three scale layers? A genuine feature must stand out both spatially (8 same-scale neighbours) and across scales (9 from the blurred-more layer + 9 from the blurred-less layer). Only points extreme in all 26 directions are likely to be repeatable from different viewpoints and distances.
Output of step ③: Integer candidate (x, y, σ) positions — roughly 1% of all pixels survive.

④ Refinement — three filters in sequence
Taylor expansion — nudges each candidate to its true sub-pixel/sub-scale peak (up to 5 iterations). Why: the 26-neighbor check is coarse; true extrema lie between grid points.

Contrast filter — reject if |D(x̂)| < 0.03. Why: faint extrema are just noise and are not stable across views.

Edge filter — reject if Hessian ratio r = λ_max/λ_min > 10. Why: points on a straight edge are poorly localised along it — a tiny shift produces a completely different descriptor, making them useless for matching.
input: ~1000s candidates output: ~100–500 stable keypoints (x, y, σ)
